# Sample teaching notes: Incoterms, delays and transport choices

> Sample material written for the BRIDGE demo. Replace with lecturer-approved course notes before
> any real use. Simplified for teaching; always consult the official Incoterms® 2020 rules.

## Incoterms® 2020: CIF obligations

Under CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight) the seller must contract and pay for carriage to the named
port of destination and obtain minimum insurance cover. The seller delivers the goods by placing
them on board the vessel at the port of shipment, within the agreed shipment date or period. Risk
passes to the buyer once the goods are on board, although the seller has paid freight to
destination. A delay that happens before loading — for example because the seller missed the
carrier's documentation cut-off — usually falls within the seller's sphere of control.

## Delay and liquidated-damages clauses

Contracts often add clauses that specify what happens when a party is late: notification duties,
who bears the extra cost of expedited transport, and liquidated damages (a pre-agreed amount per
day or week of delay, usually capped). Read the clause wording carefully: is the remedy automatic,
optional, or conditional on the delay being within a party's control?

## Commercial invoice and packing list

The commercial invoice states the parties, Incoterm, goods, quantities and values. The packing list
shows how the goods are packed — number of packages, weights and dimensions. Together they reveal
practical options, such as separating urgent items that are packed on their own pallet.

## Partial shipments

Many contracts allow partial deliveries with the buyer's agreement. Shipping only the critical items
by a faster mode can restore a customer's operations while keeping costs down.

## Cost–benefit comparison of transport modes

Compare options on total extra cost, arrival time, reliability and risk. Include the cost of not
acting: penalties, a customer's lost production, and damage to the relationship. Air freight is
faster and more predictable but costs more per kilogram than sea freight.

## Total landed cost

Landed cost includes the purchase price, freight, insurance, duties, handling and any extra costs
caused by disruptions. Decisions should be judged on total landed cost and business impact, not on
freight cost alone.

## Supply-chain risk identification and mitigation

Identify risks (port congestion, strikes, documentation errors), estimate likelihood and impact,
and plan mitigation: buffers, alternative routes, close monitoring and clear escalation.

## Principled negotiation (interests vs positions)

Focus on interests rather than positions, use objective criteria such as contract clauses, and
propose options that work for both sides. Maximal demands often harden positions and slow
agreement.

## Delivering bad news professionally

Inform stakeholders early, state the facts, explain what you are doing about it, give a realistic
next update time, and ask what matters most to them.
